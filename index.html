A 90 percent recall judge publishes 2 leaks a month

The arithmetic that kills LLM-as-judge review pipelines sits in one ratio nobody writes down: misses per true catch. Take a judge model that screens internal documents for sensitive data before publication, tuned to a recall of 0.9. That number sounds like a passing grade. It means the judge catches 18 of every 20 true leaks. Feed it 40,000 documents a month when 1 in 2,000 holds a genuine leak and the pool contains 20, so the miss line reads 2: two documents no human ever saw, published with the pipeline marked green. Precision prices the noise side. At 0.5 precision, every true catch arrives with one false positive beside it: 18 false flags riding along with the 18 catches, 36 escalations total, half of them pure noise the reviewers still have to read.

The reviewer ledger prices the trade. Escalation is the only line of defense behind the judge and every flag triggers it: 36 escalations a month at 45 minutes each is 27 reviewer-hours, which at a loaded $58 an hour is $1,566 a month to stand next to the 2 published leaks. The audit trail that makes the judge measurable costs more up front: evaluating the judge on a labeled set of 1,000 documents at 5 minutes each is 83.3 hours, $4,833 once, and that bill is the only reason recall is a measurement instead of a vendor claim. The alternative of firing the judge and hand-reading everything reads: 39,964 unflagged documents at 45 minutes is 29,973 reviewer-hours a month, about $1.74 million, $20.9 million a year. The judge does not make review cheap. It makes the 2 published leaks explicit, an acceptably priced hole instead of an invisible one.

The exit is the base rate, not the model: fix the upstream pool with cheap deterministic filters before the judge sees anything. Cut the 40,000 screened documents to 4,000 candidates and the same 0.9 recall judge misses 0.2 documents a month instead of 2, a tenfold cut bought with filters that cost nothing per document. That is the shape of every honest eval: paraphrase-drift scores and detector false-positive rates are the audit math, the labeled set is the receipt, and the misses-per-catch ratio is the number to put on the wall.
